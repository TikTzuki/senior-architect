# Rate Limiting

**Rule: without a limit, one client's mistake becomes everyone's outage.** A rate limit is not
hostility toward consumers — it is the contract that lets you keep serving all of them.

## The anti-pattern

```python
@app.get("/orders")            # no limit of any kind
def list_orders(): ...
```

The client everyone hoped for arrives, integrates enthusiastically, and polls this endpoint in a
tight loop from twenty workers. Their bug consumes your entire database capacity, and **every other
customer is now down** — including the ones who did nothing wrong.

> "Just pay and resources are unlimited."

That belief is what an unlimited API teaches consumers. There is no such thing: capacity is finite,
and the only question is whether it is allocated deliberately or taken by whoever loops fastest.

## Why development hides it

One client, generated by you, calling at human pace. There is no second tenant to be starved, no
retry loop, no misconfigured cron, and no scraper.

The failure is a **fairness** problem, and fairness cannot exist with one consumer. It appears the
first time a consumer's behaviour is not what you imagined — which is always, eventually.

## Capacity is finite in more ways than CPU

Rate limiting protects several resources, and only the first is obvious:

- **Compute and database** — the direct cost.
- **Cost** — per-request spend on third-party APIs, LLM tokens, egress, storage. Here a runaway
  client produces a bill rather than an outage, which is often worse because nothing alerts.
- **Downstream quota** — your provider limits *you*. One tenant burning your shared quota takes the
  capability away from every tenant.
- **Operational capacity** — humans. Every incident consumes attention that is not elastic.

So limits belong on anything scarce, not only on anything slow.

## The algorithms

| Algorithm             | Behaviour                                     | Use when                                                                   |
|-----------------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Fixed window**      | Count per calendar window                     | Simplest; allows a 2× burst across a boundary                              |
| **Sliding window**    | Count over a rolling interval                 | Smooths the boundary burst; more state                                     |
| **Token bucket**      | Tokens refill at a rate; a request spends one | **Best default** — allows bursts up to bucket size, sustained rate bounded |
| **Leaky bucket**      | Queue drains at a fixed rate                  | Smooth output to a fragile downstream                                      |
| **Concurrency limit** | N in flight, not N per second                 | Long or variable requests, where duration matters more than count          |

Two notes worth carrying. **Fixed windows have a real boundary flaw**: a client can send its full
allowance at 10:59:59 and again at 11:00:00, delivering 2× the limit in a moment. And **a concurrency
limit is often what you actually want** — for expensive endpoints, three simultaneous heavy reports
matters more than requests-per-minute, and it maps directly onto pool capacity (see
[connection-pools-and-latency](connection-pools-and-latency.md)).

Token bucket is the right default because bursts are legitimate: a client syncing after downtime
should be allowed to catch up, then be held to the sustained rate.

## What to key on

The key determines who is isolated from whom, and getting it wrong makes the limit useless:

- **API key / tenant** — the correct default for a business API. Each customer gets their own budget.
- **User id** — for user-facing APIs where one tenant has many users.
- **IP** — only for unauthenticated traffic. It is a poor identity: NAT puts thousands of users
  behind one address, and attackers rotate addresses freely.
- **Endpoint + key** — so a cheap endpoint's traffic cannot exhaust the budget for an expensive one.

**Tiered limits are the honest model** for a multi-tenant API: free, standard, and enterprise get
different budgets, stated in the contract. That converts "we throttled you" from an argument into a
plan.

And protect the pre-authentication surface separately. Login, signup, password reset, and token
endpoints need **stricter** limits keyed on IP *and* account, because they are abuse targets and the
attacker has no API key to limit.

## Not all endpoints cost the same

A flat requests-per-minute limit treats a keyed lookup and a five-year report as equivalent. They are
not.

**Cost-weighted limiting** charges a request what it costs — a lookup costs 1, a filtered list costs
10, a report costs 500. This is how you avoid the two failure modes of a flat limit: either it is
loose enough for the expensive endpoint to hurt you, or tight enough to make cheap calls
unnecessarily painful.

Where you can, put the expensive shapes behind an [async operation](api-async-operations.md) instead
and rate-limit *submissions*. That gives a bounded queue rather than a rejected request, which is a
better answer for both sides.

## Responding to a limited client

Rejecting is not enough; the client needs to know what to do:

```http
429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: 30
RateLimit-Limit: 1000
RateLimit-Remaining: 0
RateLimit-Reset: 1756640430

{ "error": { "code": "rate_limit_exceeded",
             "message": "Limit of 1000 requests/hour exceeded.",
             "retry_after": 30 } }
```

- **`429`, not `503`.** `503` says *I am broken*; `429` says *you are over budget*. Consumers, and
  their circuit breakers, treat those differently — and a `503` will trip a
  [circuit breaker](circuit-breakers-and-bulkheads.md) that should not be tripping.
- **Always send `Retry-After`.** Without it, a well-meaning client retries immediately and makes it
  worse.
- **Expose remaining budget on successful responses**, so clients can self-throttle before being
  rejected. This is the difference between a limit clients cooperate with and one they collide with.
- **Never let a `429` be silent.** If a client is being limited persistently, tell someone — the same
  reasoning as failing webhook deliveries.

## Distributed counting

With N instances, a per-instance counter gives N× the intended limit. Options:

- **Shared counter in Redis** — accurate, and adds a network hop plus a dependency to every request.
  Use atomic increments with expiry; a `GET`-then-`SET` is a race, per
  [transaction-isolation](transaction-isolation.md).
- **Local counters with limit/N** — no dependency, and unfair under uneven load balancing.
- **Local with periodic sync** — approximate, cheap, and usually good enough.
- **At the edge** — API gateway, CDN, or service mesh. Rejects before consuming your capacity at all,
  which is the strongest form.

The essential design decision: **fail open or fail closed** when the limiter's store is unavailable.
Failing closed turns a Redis blip into a total outage; failing open leaves you unprotected during
exactly the incident where protection matters. For most APIs, fail open with a conservative local
fallback and alert loudly.

## Rate limiting is not load shedding

Two related tools, often conflated:

- **Rate limiting** is *fairness*, applied per client, mostly independent of current load. A tenant
  over budget is rejected even on an idle system.
- **Load shedding** is *survival*, applied when the system is overloaded, regardless of whose budget
  remains. It rejects work you cannot complete in time.

You need both. Rate limits alone will not save you from legitimate aggregate traffic; shedding alone
lets one tenant starve the others. And shed **before** the queue is deep — work whose client has
already timed out is capacity spent on nothing, which is the same waste as
[caching](caching.md)'s stampede.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Every public endpoint has a rate limit; there is no unlimited path
- [ ] Limits are keyed on tenant/API key, not only IP
- [ ] Auth endpoints (login, signup, reset) have stricter limits on IP **and** account
- [ ] Expensive endpoints are limited by cost weight or concurrency, not flat request count
- [ ] Limits are counted correctly across instances (shared store or gateway)
- [ ] Fail-open vs fail-closed on limiter unavailability is a deliberate, documented choice
- [ ] `429` is returned — not `503` — with `Retry-After`
- [ ] Remaining budget is exposed on successful responses
- [ ] Limits and tiers are documented as part of the API contract
- [ ] Persistent limiting of a client raises an alert or notification
- [ ] Load shedding exists in addition to rate limiting, and sheds before the queue is deep
- [ ] Internal and machine clients are limited too, with their own budgets
